## 2. Executive Summary

### Headline Finding
> Instructional coaching effectively scaffolds primary STEM teachers in adopting dialogic strategies through structured goal-setting, video-based feedback, and concrete talk scaffolds, yet persistent language barriers (English proficiency) and entrenched teacher-centered habits continue to impede full implementation, particularly in DLP contexts.

Across the coaching corpus, a consistent pattern emerges: coaching sessions that combine student-centred goal identification, specific feedback, and shared resources (e.g., sentence frames, CPA methodology, talk phones) catalyse incremental shifts toward dialogic practice. Teachers like Joshua and Habib report experimenting with think-pair-share, wait time, and cold calling, leading to observable increases in student talk and engagement, including quieter students participating more. However, the DLP context introduces a persistent tension: students' limited English vocabulary and confidence frequently trigger reliance on native language translation (Malay/Iban), undermining sustained English dialogic talk. Coaches and teachers alike acknowledge this barrier, with some teachers initially believing that student attainment is limited by English proficiency—a belief that coaching seeks to reframe through scaffolding strategies. Longitudinal progression is evident: early sessions focus on goal-setting and awareness, mid-programme sessions introduce specific talk scaffolds, and later reflections show teachers internalising the need to adapt lesson pace and prioritise student understanding over rigid curriculum adherence. Despite progress, barriers remain: teacher-dominated talk (up to 80% in some cases), insufficient wait time, and competing priorities like exams and lesson planning divert focus from dialogic strategies. Overall, coaching is a catalyst for pedagogical change, but its impact is moderated by language challenges and the depth of teacher reflection on underlying beliefs.

## 3. Evaluation Strands

### Coaching Scaffolding and Goal Setting
Coaching consistently employs student-centred goal identification, specific feedback, and resource sharing, which supports teachers in shifting toward dialogic practice. Video observation enables focused, targeted coaching conversations.

- Coaches identify student-centred goals and check in on previous goals, creating accountability and continuity.
- Specific feedback and shared resources (e.g., CPA method, AI lessons) are used to model dialogic strategies.
- Video observation allows coaches to provide real, focused, and targeted responses, enhancing the precision of feedback.

### Adoption of Dialogic Strategies
Teachers actively experiment with and adopt structured talk strategies—think-pair-share, wait time, cold call, talk partners, and sentence frames—leading to increased student participation and engagement.

- Teachers report using wait time, cold call, and talk partners to distribute talk and include less confident students.
- Think-pair-share and gallery walks with sticky notes foster higher-order thinking and student-led explanation.
- Creative tools like 'walkie-talkie' and 'talk phones' spike engagement and focus student attention.
- Teachers notice that adapting lesson pace to student understanding is more important than strictly following the scheme of work.

### Language Barriers and Scaffolding in DLP
English proficiency is a significant barrier to dialogic talk, leading to translation dependency. Coaches and teachers employ scaffolding tools—sentence frames, pre-teaching vocabulary, and structured outputs—to build confidence and reduce reliance on native language.

- Students frequently retreat to Malay/Iban when comprehension stalls, limiting sustained English dialogue.
- Teachers use laminated sentence frames, pre-teaching of key vocabulary, and '3-word limits' to scaffold English explanations.
- Mixed-ability pairing and peer support help weaker students articulate reasoning in English.
- Coaches explicitly discuss avoiding translation and using scaffolding to support English talk.

### Persistent Pedagogical Barriers
Despite coaching, barriers such as teacher-centered habits, insufficient wait time, diverse student needs, and curriculum constraints impede full dialogic implementation.

- Teacher talk often dominates (up to 80%), and some teachers use the whiteboard to maintain control.
- Wait time of 30 seconds is insufficient; teachers acknowledge the need for longer pauses (1 minute) for conceptual processing.
- Group dynamics can be uneven—dominant pupils lead while weaker ones copy or disengage.
- Exams and lesson planning divert focus from dialogic strategies, and some teachers struggle to articulate goals.

## 6. Synthesis: Strengths & Risks to Impact

### Evidence-Backed Strengths
- Coaching consistently uses student-centred goal-setting and specific feedback, creating a structured pathway for pedagogical change.
- Video observation enables targeted, evidence-based coaching conversations that directly address classroom practice.
- Teachers are actively experimenting with a range of dialogic strategies (think-pair-share, wait time, cold call) and reporting observable increases in student participation and confidence.
- Scaffolding tools such as sentence frames, pre-teaching vocabulary, and mixed-ability pairing are effectively reducing translation dependency and building English talk capacity.
- Reflective practice is emerging, with teachers like Joshua recognising the importance of adapting lesson pace to student understanding over rigid curriculum adherence.

### Risks to Impact & Emerging Challenges
- Persistent language barriers (English proficiency) and translation dependency may limit the depth of dialogic talk in English, potentially undermining STEM reasoning in DLP contexts.
- Entrenched teacher-centered habits (e.g., 80% teacher talk, whiteboard control) and beliefs that student attainment is limited by English proficiency may resist change without deeper reflective work.
- Competing priorities such as exams and lesson planning divert focus from dialogic strategies, and some teachers struggle to articulate goals, indicating a need for ongoing coaching support.
- Insufficient wait time (30 seconds) and uneven group dynamics (dominant pupils, passive others) can reduce the effectiveness of dialogic activities if not explicitly managed.
